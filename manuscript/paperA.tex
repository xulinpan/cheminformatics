\subsection*{Abstract}
\small
Machine-learning methods for tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS) have moved away from
fixed-width binned intensity vectors towards models that read peaks at full
precision, and the resulting gains are commonly attributed to the spectral
representation. That attribution has never been tested, because abandoning bins
forces an architecture change at the same time: a peak set cannot be read by the
network that reads a binned vector. Here we separate them with a rounding control
that snaps each peak to its bin centre, merges the collisions, and passes the result
through the \emph{identical} peak-set Transformer at an identical parameter count,
so each of the encoder and the mass axis --- the precision of the $m/z$ scale ---
changes with the other held constant. On an openly licensed corpus of $414{,}992$ spectra from GNPS, MassBank
and MoNA spanning $66$ instrument types, over $10{,}058$ held-out molecules and
three seeds, replacing a $0.5$~Da binned baseline with a full-precision peak-set
model raised macro AUPRC from $0.1130$ to $0.1709$ (chance $0.0085$). The control splits that paired gain of $+0.0579$ into $+0.0438$ ($95\%$ CI
$+0.0364$ to $+0.0517$) for the encoder and $+0.0142$ ($+0.0097$ to $+0.0188$) for
the mass axis: $76\%$ is architectural, and the ranking encoder $>$ aggregation $>$
mass axis holds in every seed. Repeating the ladder on a single-library corpus that is
$99\%$ one instrument gives the encoder $58\%$.
Improvements measured against binned baselines should not be read as evidence about
spectral representation. Code:
\url{https://github.com/xulinpan/cheminformatics}.
\normalsize

\hd{Scientific contribution}
\small
We introduce a rounding control that isolates the spectral representation from the
encoder in MS/MS substructure prediction. Existing benchmarks compare featurizations
bundled with the encoders that read them, because changing the representation
ordinarily forces an architecture change; the control removes that coupling. Across three seeds and two corpora of sharply different acquisition
diversity, the encoder accounts for $76\%$ and $58\%$ of the gain a two-arm
comparison credits to the representation, and is in both cases the largest of the
three effects we measure. The consequence for practice is that performance measured
against a binned baseline confounds representation with architecture and cannot
support claims about either alone.
\normalsize

\section*{Introduction}

Assigning a chemical structure to an MS/MS spectrum is the rate-limiting step in
untargeted metabolomics. Where the molecule appears in a reference library the
problem reduces to spectral matching, but most spectra acquired from biological
extracts do not \cite{hoffmann2022cosmic}. For the remainder, computational
annotation relates the spectrum to candidate structures: by in-silico fragmentation
and scoring, as in MetFrag \cite{ruttkies2016metfrag}; by predicting a molecular
fingerprint and searching a structure database, as in CSI:FingerID
\cite{duhrkop2015csifingerid} and MIST \cite{goldman2023mist}; or by learning a
spectrum representation whose geometry reflects chemical similarity, as in
MS2DeepScore \cite{huber2021ms2deepscore} and DreaMS \cite{bushuiev2025dreams}.

For most of the past decade these systems took the same input object. The spectrum,
physically a sparse list of $(m/z, \text{intensity})$ pairs, was discretised onto a
fixed grid of bins and handed to the model as a dense vector. The convention came
from spectral library search, where a bin comfortably wider than the instrument's
mass error makes cosine matching robust to calibration drift between laboratories.
A $0.5$~Da bin spans roughly $1{,}700$~ppm at $m/z\,300$ while a modern
time-of-flight instrument delivers about $5$~ppm, so everything the instrument
resolves inside that window is discarded --- including the mass defect, the
difference between a fragment's exact and nominal mass, which distinguishes
$\mathrm{C_5H_7^+}$ ($67.0542$) from $\mathrm{C_4H_3O^+}$ ($67.0178$) and therefore
carries elemental composition.

The field has since moved away from naive binning. CSI:FingerID scores against
fragmentation trees over exact masses, MIST tokenises peaks by chemical formula,
DreaMS operates on peak lists, and TransExION aligns fragments by nominal mass with
the defect embedded as a separate channel \cite{buithi2024transexion}; the
direction is surveyed in \cite{beck2024mlms}, and the convention of discretising a
spectrum onto a fixed grid persists well beyond MS/MS \cite{tan2025clams}, and how
best to present a spectrum to a model remains open enough that serialising peaks as
text for a language model is an active line of enquiry \cite{wang2026llm}. These
systems outperform binned baselines, and the improvement is routinely read as
evidence that the representation was the problem.

Binning has not gone unexamined. Gin\'e et al. \cite{gine2026benchmark} benchmark
featurization strategies for structure annotation across a large grid of models,
comparing adaptive binning and spectrum hashing against learned embeddings, and
report that retrieval degrades markedly as the mass tolerance is relaxed. Working
on chemical-similarity prediction rather than structure annotation, de Jonge et al.
\cite{dejonge2026crossmode} settle on $0.1$~Da bins and report that finer bins did
not improve performance. These are the two most direct empirical statements
available about what mass precision is worth, and they do not agree with each other
--- which is itself a reason to ask the question in a form that admits a clean
answer.

That reading does not follow from the experiments, and the reason is structural
rather than careless. A binned vector and a peak set are different kinds of object:
the first is dense and fixed-length, the second sparse and variable-length and
permutation-invariant. No architecture reads both. Abandoning bins therefore forces
a change of encoder, and a comparison of a peak-level model against a binned
baseline varies the representation and the architecture together, usually alongside
differences in training data and objective.

This applies to benchmarks as well as to individual systems. A study that compares
many featurizations \cite{gine2026benchmark} measures each one bundled with the
encoder that reads it --- adaptive binning with a network that takes a dense
vector, a learned embedding with the architecture that produced it --- and so
estimates the joint effect of representation and architecture at many points rather
than separating them at any one. That is the right design for choosing a pipeline
and the wrong one for attributing a gain. What is missing is not breadth but a
contrast in which the mass axis moves and nothing else does.

This paper supplies the separation. The obstacle above is real but not
insurmountable: while a peak-set encoder cannot read a binned vector, it can read
peaks whose $m/z$ has been \emph{rounded to the bin grid}. Quantising the mass axis
--- we use the term throughout for the precision of the $m/z$ scale, the component
of the input representation that binning actually destroys --- while leaving the
peak-set interface intact yields an arm that carries the baseline's mass
information through the treatment's architecture, and inserting it between the two
turns a confounded two-arm comparison into a decomposition. We
report that decomposition across three training seeds, extend it with a bin-width
sweep that prices precision as a continuous quantity, and examine what the results
imply for how baselines in this area should be constructed and read.

\section*{Methods}

\hd{Corpora and splits}
Both corpora are drawn from the CASMI 2026 training corpus, an aggregation of
eleven public and in-house MS/MS libraries whose \texttt{ingest\_lib} column records
each spectrum's source.

Our primary corpus is the openly licensed subset: every spectrum contributed by
GNPS, MassBank or MoNA, $414{,}992$ spectra over $52{,}428$ structures, spanning
$66$ instrument types and $112$ adducts. We use it because it is the part of the
corpus a third party can obtain from its sources without an account, and because it
is acquisitionally diverse.

Our secondary corpus is a contiguous slice of two of the file's twenty-one row
groups, $262{,}144$ spectra. Because the file is ordered by source library, that
slice is not a sample of the corpus: it is $99.0\%$ one in-house library and $1.0\%$
another, and $99\%$ of its spectra come from a single instrument (timsTOF), across
two instrument types and sixteen adducts. We report it not as a second sample but as
a deliberately contrasting one --- a near-homogeneous corpus against a heterogeneous
one --- which is what makes the comparison in Fig.~\ref{fig:corpora} informative.

Spectra were retained if the precursor $m/z$ was below $2{,}000$ and the reported
precursor error within $20$~ppm; peaks were retained above $0.1\%$ of the base peak
and below the precursor plus $1.5$~Da, capped at the $256$ most intense, with $m/z$
held in double precision throughout preprocessing. On the open corpus this leaves
$372{,}200$ spectra over $50{,}287$ structures and $24{,}627{,}256$ peaks.

Structures were assigned to five folds by grouping generic Murcko scaffolds, with
acyclic structures falling back to grouping by their own identifier, and fold sizes
balanced greedily. Scaffold grouping is stricter than a random split and follows the
generalisation-demanding practice argued for in MassSpecGym
\cite{bushuiev2024massspecgym}; we did not adopt that benchmark's own split because
our corpora are selected by source library rather than drawn from it. Folds 2--4 were used for fitting, fold 1 for model selection, and
fold 0 was held out; all reported numbers are on fold 0 ($10{,}058$ molecules on the
open corpus, $8{,}692$ on the single-library corpus).

\hd{Structural targets}
Each structure was decomposed with BRICS and represented by the presence or absence
of each of the $512$ most frequent attachment-labelled fragments, the vocabulary
fitted on the training folds alone. On the open corpus the target matrix is $99.3\%$
zero with a median of $4$ positive targets per molecule; $433$ targets carry at
least five held-out positives, at a mean prevalence of $0.0085$, which is the chance
level for macro AUPRC. The single-library corpus is less sparse ($97.1\%$ zero,
median $17$, $477$ scored targets, chance $0.0093$), reflecting how much more
chemical variety three public libraries carry than one in-house library. Because
almost all targets fall below $1\%$ prevalence we report AUPRC as the primary metric
and AUROC only as a secondary summary.

\hd{The four arms}
The design is a ladder in which each arm differs from the one below it in exactly
one respect.

\emph{M0, the conventional baseline.} Each spectrum is projected onto $2{,}048$
fragment bins and $2{,}048$ precursor-minus-fragment bins of width $0.5$~Da over
$[0, 1024)$, with square-root intensity accumulated into each bin and the
concatenated vector $L_2$-normalised. The $4{,}096$-dimensional vector is encoded by
a two-layer perceptron. This is the representation used, with minor variation in bin
width, throughout the MS/MS machine-learning literature.

\emph{M1R, the rounding control.} Each peak's $m/z$ is assigned to a cell by
$\lfloor m/w \rfloor$ with $w = 0.5$~Da --- the same indexing M0's encoder uses ---
and placed at that cell's centre. Peaks sharing a cell are merged by accumulating
square-root intensity, the accumulator M0 applies, so the merged peak carries the
total M0 would have seen. The resulting tokens pass through the peak-set encoder
described below. The arm therefore holds mass information at M0's resolution while
using M1's architecture.

One detail is load-bearing. The encoder derives the neutral loss as
$m^{\mathrm{prec}} - m$. Quantising the peaks while leaving the precursor at full
precision would return the precursor's mass defect to every token through that
subtraction, silently defeating the control, so the precursor is snapped to the same
grid whenever quantisation is active. A unit test asserts that two precursors in one
cell give identical embeddings under the control and different embeddings without
it.

\emph{M1, full precision.} Identical to M1R with quantisation disabled, so peaks
reach the encoder at the precision the instrument reported. M1R and M1 are identical
in every other configuration field and have identical parameter counts
($712{,}336$); a test asserts both.

\emph{M2, hierarchical aggregation.} Identical to M1 except that mean pooling over
a molecule's spectra is replaced by a measurement model weighting each spectrum by a
learned, acquisition-dependent precision. This arm is included to give the other two
effects a scale, not as a contribution of this paper.

\hd{Peak encoding}
Each peak is encoded as a token. For a peak at $m/z$ $m$ in a spectrum with
precursor $m^{\mathrm{prec}}$ we form a multi-scale sinusoidal embedding
\begin{equation}
\varphi_L(m) = \bigl[\sin(2\pi m/\lambda_1), \cos(2\pi m/\lambda_1), \dots,
\sin(2\pi m/\lambda_L), \cos(2\pi m/\lambda_L)\bigr]^{\top},
\label{eq:fourier}
\end{equation}
with $L = 32$ wavelengths spaced geometrically from $2$~mDa to $10^{3}$~Da, applied
both to $m$ and to the neutral loss. Short wavelengths resolve the mass defect and
long wavelengths carry nominal mass. The token also receives the explicit defect
$\delta = m - \mathrm{round}(m)$, square-root and log-transformed intensity, and
intensity rank.

At a wavelength of $2$~mDa and $m/z\,342$ the phase in Eq.~\eqref{eq:fourier}
exceeds $10^{6}$, and evaluating it in single precision destroys exactly the
low-order information the embedding exists to preserve. We therefore compute the
phase in double precision and reduce it modulo $2\pi$ before casting. With this
reduction the embeddings of $\mathrm{C_4H_3O^+}$ and $\mathrm{C_5H_7^+}$, which
share a $0.5$~Da bin, differ by $1.82$ in the infinity norm.

Tokens are encoded by a four-block Transformer with a learned pooling token, each
block conditioned on the acquisition covariates (adduct, polarity, instrument
family, harmonised collision energy) by adaptive layer normalisation. The encoder is
permutation invariant and accepts variable peak counts.

\hd{Bin-width sweep}
To price precision as a continuous quantity rather than at one grid, we repeated the
M1R construction at $w \in \{0.25, 0.1, 0.05, 0.01\}$~Da, with the encoder,
parameter count, schedule and seed fixed. Together with $w = 0.5$ (M1R) and full
precision (M1) this gives six points differing only in how finely the mass axis is
resolved. The sweep was run on the single-library corpus only.

\hd{Training protocol}
Every arm was trained for fifteen epochs with AdamW, a one-cycle schedule peaking at
$10^{-3}$, batches of $16$ molecules, mixed precision and gradient clipping at
$1.0$. The loss was binary cross-entropy over the $512$ presence targets with an
auxiliary negative-binomial term on $88$ functional-group counts, and checkpoints
were selected on validation macro AUPRC. No hyperparameter search was performed for
any arm. The four ladder arms were each trained three times under seeds
$\{2026, 7, 13\}$ on each corpus --- twenty-four training runs in all --- and the
sweep arms once each.

\hd{Evaluation}
We report macro AUPRC over targets with at least five held-out positives, with macro
AUROC, micro AUPRC and Brier score as secondary summaries. Because arms are
evaluated on identical molecules and identical targets, the appropriate contrast is
paired: each target's average precision is first averaged over the three seeds, the
per-target differences are then taken, and their mean is bootstrapped over targets
($5{,}000$ resamples). We report the fraction of targets improved alongside, and,
for each effect, its standard deviation across seeds --- which, unlike the bootstrap
interval, reflects initialisation variance. Where an ordering between effects is at
issue we check it within each seed rather than reading it off the means.

\section*{Discussion}

The headline is a reattribution. A two-arm comparison on the open corpus credits the
spectral representation with $+0.0579$ macro AUPRC; once the encoder is held
constant, the representation's share is $+0.0142$ and the encoder's is $+0.0438$.
Both are real, and the smaller by a factor of three is the one the literature has
been discussing.

It is worth being clear about why this went unnoticed, because the answer is not
inattention. The confound is forced by the data structures involved. A binned vector
is dense and fixed-length; a peak set is sparse, variable-length and
permutation-invariant. No architecture reads both, so an experiment that changes only
the representation appears impossible, and the comparison everyone can run is the one
that changes both. The rounding control works not by finding a network that reads
both objects but by degrading one object into the other while keeping the interface
fixed. The manoeuvre is simple in retrospect, and we suspect it generalises: wherever
a representation change forces an architecture change, asking whether the richer
representation can be impoverished in place is likely to be more productive than
looking for a universal encoder.

Our result does not diminish the systems that abandoned binning. CSI:FingerID, MIST,
DreaMS and TransExION are better than binned baselines, and this work does not
dispute it. What it changes is the account of \emph{why}. To the extent that those
systems differ from a binned baseline in both respects, our decomposition suggests
the larger part of their advantage comes from the architectures they adopted rather
than from the mass information they preserved. That is a testable claim about each of
them, and the control we describe is cheap enough to apply.

The two corpora sharpen this and add a caveat of their own. The encoder dominates on
both, so the conclusion does not depend on a single dataset --- but its margin does.
On a corpus that is $99\%$ one high-resolution instrument the encoder takes $58\%$ of
the gain; on one spanning $66$ instrument types it takes $76\%$, and the mass axis
roughly halves. Our hypothesis is that a heterogeneous corpus contains many spectra
whose reported $m/z$ carries little real precision, so preserving precision helps
less on average. If that is right, the value of full-precision input depends on the
instrument mix a method will encounter, and a method tuned on a homogeneous
high-resolution corpus will disappoint on a heterogeneous one for reasons that have
nothing to do with its architecture. We did not test this, and say so in the
Limitations; we flag it because it bears directly on how these methods should be
benchmarked.

The sweep adds a second complication. Precision buys nothing measurable between $0.5$
and $0.1$~Da; the gain appears below $0.05$~Da and is not exhausted at $0.01$~Da. A
$10$~mDa grid separates the isobaric fragments that motivate the mass-defect
argument, yet still leaves about a third of the benefit unrealised, which suggests
the model uses the defect as a continuous quantity rather than merely as a
discriminator between candidate formulas. This sits awkwardly with the common design
choice, TransExION's included, of embedding the defect alongside a coarsely quantised
nominal mass: on our evidence the resolution of that channel matters more than its
presence.

It also disagrees with a recent result we should not paper over. de Jonge et al.
\cite{dejonge2026crossmode} adopt $0.1$~Da bins for MS2DeepScore~2.0 and report that
finer bins did not improve performance; our sweep finds nothing measurable between
$0.5$ and $0.1$~Da, agreeing with them over that range, but a gain of $+0.0121$
between $0.05$ and $0.01$~Da, disagreeing below it. Three explanations seem live.
The tasks differ --- chemical-similarity prediction between two spectra may be
satisfied by coarser features than inferring which substructures a molecule
contains, since the former needs only that two spectra of similar molecules land
near each other. The architectures differ. And our sweep is single-seed, so the
disagreement may be ours. We cannot adjudicate this from our data, and flag it
because a reader comparing the two papers will notice, and because the question of
what bin width suffices is evidently not settled by either.

The practical consequence concerns controls. A method evaluated against a binned
baseline is compared against an arm handicapped in two ways at once, and on this
evidence the larger handicap is architectural. A paper reporting a gain over such a
baseline has not shown that its representation is responsible, whatever its
discussion says. The remedy is inexpensive: report the comparator's encoder as
explicitly as its representation, and where the claim is about representation,
include an arm that changes only that.

\hd{Limitations}
The open corpus is roughly a sixth of the available spectra and the single-library
corpus a tenth; absolute values will move with scale even if the ordering does not.
Three seeds per arm establish the ordering on the open corpus, where it holds in
every seed, but on the single-library corpus the mass axis and aggregation could not
be separated, and we declined to rank them there. The sweep carries one run per width
and was run on one corpus, so only its endpoints should be read as findings and its
$0.05$~Da threshold may not transfer. The instrument-diversity hypothesis above is
untested: stratifying the mass-axis effect by instrument resolution would settle it
and is the most informative experiment this work leaves undone. Finally, and most
substantively, M1R does not separate dense from sparse encoding: a Transformer over
bin-indexed tokens is not a perceptron over a dense vector, and some of what we
attribute to the encoder may be that switch rather than the attention mechanism. A
fifth arm --- a perceptron over the rounded peak set, or a Transformer over bin
tokens without merging --- would resolve it, and we regard that as the natural next
experiment rather than a caveat that undermines the present one.

\section*{Conclusion}

Binned intensity vectors entered MS/MS machine learning from spectral library search,
and the field's move away from them has been read as evidence that the spectral
representation was the limiting factor. That reading was never tested, because no
architecture reads both a binned vector and a peak set, so representation and encoder
have always changed together. Inserting an arm that carries binned mass information
through a peak-set encoder separates them. On an openly licensed corpus of $414{,}992$
spectra spanning $66$ instrument types, across three seeds and $10{,}058$ held-out
molecules, $76\%$ of the gain attributed to the representation is the encoder
($+0.0438$) and $24\%$ is the mass axis ($+0.0142$), with the ranking encoder $>$
aggregation $>$ mass axis holding in every seed. On a near-homogeneous single-library
corpus the same decomposition gives $58\%$ and $42\%$. Mass precision matters, and
matters more the more homogeneous and higher-resolution the instruments are, but on
both corpora it matters less than the architecture adopted alongside it. Improvements
measured against binned baselines cannot be used as evidence about either.

\section*{Results}

\hd{The four arms}
The experiment is a ladder of four models in which each rung differs from the one
below it in exactly one respect (Table~\ref{tab:table1_arms}). M0 is the
conventional baseline: a $0.5$~Da binned intensity vector read by a two-layer
perceptron. M1R keeps that binned mass axis but replaces the perceptron with the
peak-set Transformer, snapping each peak to its bin centre and merging the peaks
that collide, so the encoder receives tokens carrying no more mass information
than the binned vector did. M1 hands the same Transformer the peaks at full
precision. M2 adds hierarchical pooling over a molecule's spectra. Data, targets,
splits, optimiser, schedule and evaluation are fixed throughout; M1R and M1 are
identical in every configuration field but the width of the mass grid, and carry
identical parameter counts. Each arm was trained three times under seeds
$\{2026, 7, 13\}$.

The rung that matters for the question is M1R. Without it, the contrast between a
binned baseline and a peak-set model changes the mass axis and the encoder at the
same time, and a difference between them cannot be assigned to either. M1R holds
the mass axis at the baseline's resolution while giving it the treatment arm's
encoder, so the two halves separate.

\hd{Seed spread}
Across seeds the arms are stable enough to compare, but not uniformly so
(Table~\ref{tab:table1_arms}). M0 varies by $0.0006$ and M2 by $0.0016$, while M1
varies by $0.0039$ --- roughly six times M0's spread and, as it turns out, of the
same order as the differences we wish to resolve. Any claim about the relative
size of the three effects therefore has to be checked seed by seed rather than
read off the means, and we do so below.

\hd{Most of the apparent representation gain is the encoder}
Replacing the binned vector with a full-precision peak set raises macro AUPRC from
$0.0478$ to $0.1122$, a paired per-target gain of $+0.0645$ ($95\%$ CI $+0.0576$
to $+0.0713$) improving $92.5\%$ of targets --- the effect one would report from a
two-arm comparison. The control splits it in two
(Fig.~\ref{fig:decomposition}, Table~\ref{tab:table2_effects}). Changing the
encoder alone, with the mass axis binned in both arms, is worth $+0.0377$
($+0.0331$ to $+0.0424$; $90.6\%$ of targets). Changing the mass axis alone, with
the same encoder on both sides, is worth $+0.0268$ ($+0.0221$ to $+0.0317$;
$74.8\%$ of targets). The two components sum to the joint effect
exactly, as they must, since each target's contribution enters both sides
linearly.

Fifty-eight per cent of what a two-arm comparison would attribute to the spectral
representation is therefore the encoder. Both components are real: each interval
excludes zero by a wide margin and each improves a clear majority of targets
individually. But they are not what the headline number suggests, and the larger
of the two is the one that has nothing to do with how the spectrum is represented.

\hd{What the ordering does and does not support}
The encoder is the largest of the three effects, and this survives replication: it
exceeds the mass-axis effect in all three seeds (by $+0.0139$, $+0.0154$ and
$+0.0033$) and the aggregation effect in all three (by $+0.0051$, $+0.0040$ and
$+0.0074$). We regard that as established at this sample size.

The ordering of the remaining two is not. The mass axis averages $+0.0268$ and
aggregation $+0.0322$, which would place the representation last, but the sign of
that difference reverses under one of the three seeds ($-0.0089$, $-0.0114$,
$+0.0041$). Three seeds cannot separate effects that differ by $0.0054$ when the
per-seed spread of the difference is of the same magnitude. We therefore claim
only that the mass axis and the pooling strategy are of comparable value, and that
both are smaller than the encoder. A reader wanting the two ranked will need more
replicates than we have run.

This matters for how the experiment should be read. It would be convenient to
report that the spectral representation is the smallest of the three effects; the
data do not support that, and the conservative statement --- that it is not the
largest --- is the one the replication licenses.

\hd{How much precision is needed}
The rounding control prices one particular grid. Sweeping the grid width, with the
encoder and parameter count fixed throughout, prices precision as a function of how
much of it is retained (Fig.~\ref{fig:binwidth},
Table~\ref{tab:table3_binwidth}). At $0.5$, $0.25$ and $0.1$~Da the model sits
between $0.0801$ and $0.0867$, a range of $0.0066$ --- under twice the
full-precision arm's seed standard deviation of $0.0039$, and unordered within
itself; coarsening from $0.1$ to $0.5$~Da costs nothing measurable.
Below that the curve moves: $0.05$~Da reaches $0.0909$ and $0.01$~Da reaches
$0.1030$, closing $65\%$ of the distance to full precision. Full precision reaches
$0.1119$, so even a $10$~mDa grid --- fine enough to separate the isobaric
fragments that motivate the whole argument --- leaves roughly a third of the
precision benefit on the table.

Two cautions. Each width was run once, and the non-monotonic dip at $0.25$ and
$0.1$~Da spans $0.0066$, under twice the full-precision arm's seed standard
deviation of $0.0039$ and therefore unremarkable across five single runs; we read
it as noise rather than structure. We tested whether it might
instead be an aliasing artefact --- the sinusoidal basis has wavelengths that are
integer divisors of these grid spacings, which would render some channels constant
--- but only one to three of thirty-two channels are affected at any width and the
count does not track performance ($r = -0.26$), so we discount that explanation.
Resolving the middle of this curve would need seeds at each width.

\hd{What binning removes}
The mechanism is visible before any model is fitted
(Fig.~\ref{fig:representation}). Of the $93{,}611$ evaluation peaks that survive
preprocessing and are seen by every arm, $35{,}116$ --- $37.5\%$ --- fall into a
$0.5$~Da bin already occupied by another peak from the same spectrum. The loss
falls with the grid: $33.1\%$ at $0.1$~Da, $25.9\%$ at $0.05$~Da, $7.5\%$ at
$0.01$~Da. The observed mass defect spans $[-0.47, +0.47]$~Da with clear
compositional structure, and enumerating plausible CHNOS compositions within
$10$~ppm places $328$ candidate fragment formulas inside a single $0.5$~Da bin at
$m/z\,300$.

The denominator deserves a note, because the obvious one is misleading. The raw
test files contain $400{,}707$ peaks, of which $60.7\%$ collide under $0.5$~Da
binning. Preprocessing removes $76.6\%$ of those as sub-threshold before any arm is
trained, and the removed peaks are disproportionately the dense low-intensity ones
that collide. Quoting the raw figure would charge binning for peaks no model ever
receives, so we report absorption over the cleaned spectra throughout.

Comparing the absorption curve with the performance curve is instructive, and
slightly deflationary. Going from $0.5$ to $0.1$~Da removes about a tenth of the
absorption and buys nothing measurable; going from $0.05$ to $0.01$~Da removes
about two thirds of the remainder and buys $+0.0121$. Peak absorption is therefore
not a sufficient account of the cost on its own: what the model gains below
$0.05$~Da tracks the recovery of the defect value itself more closely than it
tracks the recovery of peak count.

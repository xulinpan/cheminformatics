% Results for Paper A. Primary corpus: GNPS + MassBank + MoNA (open, 66 instrument
% types). Secondary: the single-library corpus, which supplies a replication under
% very different acquisition diversity plus the bin-width sweep.
\section*{Results}

\hd{The four arms}
The design is a ladder in which each arm differs from the one below it in exactly
one respect (Table~\ref{tab:table1_arms_open}). M0 is the conventional baseline: a
$0.5$~Da binned intensity vector read by a two-layer perceptron. M1R keeps that
binned mass axis but replaces the perceptron with the peak-set Transformer, snapping
each peak to its bin centre and merging the peaks that collide, so the encoder
receives tokens carrying no more mass information than the binned vector did. M1
hands the same Transformer the peaks at full precision. M2 adds hierarchical pooling
over a molecule's spectra. M1R and M1 are identical in every configuration field but
the width of the mass grid, and carry identical parameter counts. Each arm was
trained three times, under seeds $\{2026, 7, 13\}$.

M1R is the rung that makes the experiment mean anything. Without it, the contrast
between a binned baseline and a peak-set model changes the mass axis and the encoder
together, and a difference between them cannot be assigned to either.

\hd{Three quarters of the apparent representation gain is the encoder}
Replacing the binned vector with a full-precision peak set raises macro AUPRC from
$0.1130$ to $0.1709$ against a chance level of $0.0085$, a paired per-target gain of
$+0.0579$ ($95\%$ CI $+0.0494$ to $+0.0667$) improving $81.8\%$ of targets --- the
effect a two-arm comparison would report. The control splits it
(Fig.~\ref{fig:decomposition}, Table~\ref{tab:table2_effects_open}). Changing the
encoder alone, with the mass axis binned on both sides, is worth $+0.0438$
($+0.0364$ to $+0.0517$; $79.2\%$ of targets). Changing the mass axis alone, with
the same encoder on both sides, is worth $+0.0142$ ($+0.0097$ to $+0.0188$; $64.9\%$
of targets). The two components sum to the joint effect exactly, as they must, since
each target's contribution enters both sides linearly.

Seventy-six per cent of what a two-arm comparison would credit to the spectral
representation is therefore the encoder. Both components are real --- each interval
excludes zero and each improves a clear majority of targets --- but the larger by a
factor of three is the one that has nothing to do with how the spectrum is
represented.

\hd{The ordering is stable across seeds}
All three pairwise orderings hold in every seed. The encoder effect exceeds the
mass-axis effect by $+0.0331$, $+0.0288$ and $+0.0268$, and the aggregation effect
by $+0.0303$, $+0.0243$ and $+0.0231$; aggregation exceeds the mass axis by
$+0.0028$, $+0.0045$ and $+0.0038$. The ranking encoder $>$ aggregation $>$ mass
axis therefore holds three times out of three, and the mass-axis effect is the most
stable of the three across seeds (SD $0.0004$, against $0.0030$ for the encoder).

That the smallest effect is also the most reproducible is worth stating plainly: the
mass axis is not a marginal effect lost in noise, it is a small effect measured
precisely.

\hd{The same decomposition on a very different corpus}
Because the finding is a claim about how the field should construct comparisons
rather than about one dataset, we repeated the entire ladder on a second corpus with
almost nothing in common with the first: a single in-house library, $99\%$ of whose
spectra come from one instrument (timsTOF), against the open corpus's $66$
instrument types drawn from three public libraries
(Fig.~\ref{fig:corpora}, Table~\ref{tab:table3_two_corpora}).

The qualitative result is unchanged --- the encoder dominates on both --- but the
balance shifts. On the single-library corpus the encoder accounts for $58.5\%$ of
the joint gain; on the open corpus, $75.5\%$. The mass-axis effect falls from
$+0.0268$ to $+0.0142$, roughly halving, while the encoder effect grows slightly
from $+0.0377$ to $+0.0438$.

The single-library corpus also leaves one ordering unresolved that the open corpus
settles. There, the mass axis averaged $+0.0268$ against aggregation's $+0.0322$,
but the sign of that difference reversed under one of three seeds, and we could not
rank them. On the open corpus the same comparison holds in all three seeds.

\hd{What binning removes, and how much of it matters}
The mechanism is visible before any model is fitted. Of the $3{,}797{,}166$ peaks in
the open corpus's held-out spectra, $23.6\%$ fall into a $0.5$~Da bin already
occupied by another peak from the same spectrum and are therefore absorbed. On the
single-library corpus's held-out spectra the same figure is $34.4\%$
(Fig.~\ref{fig:absorption}). The loss falls with the grid on both, reaching $2.9\%$
and $6.5\%$ respectively at $0.01$~Da.

What a merged peak costs is compositional identity. Enumerating plausible CHNOS
compositions within $10$~ppm places $328$ candidate fragment formulas inside a
single $0.5$~Da bin at $m/z\,300$; the count is governed by the width of the mass
window in the way Kind and Fiehn established for precursor formulas
\cite{kind2007rules}, so a bin three orders of magnitude wider than the
instrument's error discards essentially all of the discrimination the measurement
bought.

The two corpora therefore differ in the same direction on both quantities: the one
that loses more peaks to binning is also the one where the mass axis is worth more
($34.4\%$ and $+0.0268$; $23.6\%$ and $+0.0142$). The correspondence is not
proportional --- absorption falls to $0.69$ of its value while the effect falls to
$0.53$ --- so peak absorption is not a complete account of the cost, but it moves
with it.

We offer one hypothesis for the difference, which our design does not test. The
single-library corpus is almost entirely timsTOF, a high-resolution instrument on
which every reported $m/z$ carries real precision. The open corpus spans $66$
instrument types of varying resolution, and on the lower-resolution ones there is
less precision present to preserve, so the average value of preserving it falls.
Stratifying the mass-axis effect by instrument resolution would test this directly
and we regard it as the most informative experiment this work leaves undone.

\hd{How much precision is needed}
The rounding control prices one particular grid. Sweeping the grid width on the
single-library corpus, with the encoder and parameter count fixed throughout, prices
precision as a function of how much of it is retained
(Table~\ref{tab:table3_binwidth}). At $0.5$, $0.25$ and $0.1$~Da the model sits
between $0.0801$ and $0.0867$, a range of $0.0066$ --- under twice that arm's seed
standard deviation of $0.0039$, and unordered within itself; coarsening from $0.1$
to $0.5$~Da costs nothing measurable. Below that the curve moves: $0.05$~Da reaches
$0.0909$ and $0.01$~Da reaches $0.1030$, closing $65\%$ of the distance to full
precision. Even a $10$~mDa grid --- fine enough to separate the isobaric fragments
that motivate the mass-defect argument --- leaves roughly a third of the benefit
unrealised, which suggests the model uses the defect as a continuous quantity rather
than merely as a discriminator between candidate formulas.

Two cautions. Each width was run once, so the non-monotonic dip at $0.25$ and
$0.1$~Da, spanning $0.0066$, is unremarkable across five single runs and we read it
as noise rather than structure. We tested whether it might instead be an aliasing
artefact --- the sinusoidal basis has wavelengths that are integer divisors of these
grid spacings, which would render some channels constant --- but only one to three
of thirty-two channels are affected at any width and the count does not track
performance ($r = -0.26$), so we discount that explanation. The sweep was run on the
single-library corpus only; whether the $0.05$~Da threshold moves with instrument
diversity is open.

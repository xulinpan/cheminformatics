\hd{What these intervals do and do not cover}
Each interval resamples targets, having first averaged average precision over the
three seeds. It therefore describes uncertainty from the finite set of targets, not
from initialisation. Seed variability is reported separately as the SD column of
Table~\ref{tab:s2}, and the two should be read together: on the single-library
corpus the mass-axis effect has a seed SD of $0.0049$, comparable to the width of
its bootstrap interval, which is the statistical form of the ordering failure in
Table~\ref{tab:s3}. A variance-components treatment with targets and seeds as
crossed random effects would fold both into one interval and we regard it as the
right analysis for a larger replicate count.

\hd{The attention arm}
M1D removes self-attention from every block while holding the mass axis, the peak
tokens and the acquisition conditioning fixed. Two further things change with it.
A learned pooling token cannot aggregate position-wise tokens once attention is
gone, so M1D pools by a masked mean; and removing attention frees $66{,}048$ weights
per block, so the feed-forward is widened to $548$ units in this arm alone to keep
the arm from also being the smaller model ($714{,}528$ parameters against
$712{,}336$). $\mathrm{M1R}-\mathrm{M1D}$ therefore compares two configurations
rather than pricing attention on its own, and the main text reports it as such. An
exact ablation holding pooling and feed-forward width fixed on both sides is the
experiment that would price attention, and it has not been run. What can be said
from these runs is that this contrast is the only one in the study whose interval
includes zero, and the only one positive in fewer than three seeds of three.

\section*{S5\quad Absorption by acquisition condition}

The main text reports that $23.6\%$ of the open corpus's held-out peaks fall into a
$0.5$~Da bin already occupied. Figure~\ref{fig:s2} breaks that down by adduct,
collision energy and instrument family.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/figS2_absorption_strata.pdf}
\caption{Absorption at $0.5$~Da by acquisition stratum, over the open corpus's
held-out spectra. Strata with fewer than $20{,}000$ peaks are omitted.}
\label{fig:s2}
\end{figure}

Absorption tracks peak density rather than any chemical property of the adduct. The
highest rate among common adducts is $[2\mathrm{M}-\mathrm{H}]^-$ at $48.7\%$,
whose spectra are dimeric and correspondingly crowded; $[\mathrm{M}+\mathrm{H}]^+$,
which supplies $62\%$ of held-out spectra, sits at $22.8\%$. Across collision
energies the range is $16\%$ to $26\%$ with no monotone trend. Orbitrap spectra lose
$24.9\%$ against QTOF's $20.8\%$, again following density ($52.6$ against $47.2$
peaks per spectrum) rather than resolving power.

This is worth stating because it constrains the interpretation in the main text.
Binning costs more where peaks are dense, and density varies with acquisition
conditions that have nothing to do with how much mass precision the instrument
actually delivered.

\section*{S8\quad The two controls in full}

Both controls are released as \texttt{msms-controls}, a NumPy-only library under
the MIT licence. They are short enough to state here as well, so that the method
does not depend on a repository still resolving. Neither is specific to this
model, this corpus or this deep-learning framework.

\hd{The rounding control}
Given one spectrum's peaks $(m_k, I_k)$, its precursor $m^{\mathrm{prec}}$, and the
grid width $w$ used by the binned baseline:

\begin{verbatim}
  # 1. assign peaks to the baseline's cells, and move each to its centre
  c_k    = floor(m_k / w)
  m_out  = (unique(c_k) + 0.5) * w

  # 2. merge the peaks that now share a cell, using the accumulator the
  #    baseline uses. For a sqrt-intensity binned vector that is
  I_out[c] = ( sum of sqrt(I_k) over peaks k with c_k = c ) ** 2

  # 3. snap the precursor to the SAME grid
  m_prec_out = (floor(m_prec / w) + 0.5) * w
\end{verbatim}

The resulting peaks pass through the unmodified peak-set encoder. No peak is added
or removed; only the mass axis is degraded and colliding peaks are combined.

Step 3 is the one that is easy to omit and costly to omit. Most peak encoders
derive a neutral loss as $m^{\mathrm{prec}} - m_k$. If the peaks are quantised
while the precursor keeps full precision, that subtraction returns the precursor's
mass defect to every token, and the arm intended to carry only binned mass
information silently regains a full-precision channel. The control then
understates the value of the mass axis, and nothing in the training curves
reveals it. A unit test asserts that two precursors falling in one cell give
identical embeddings under the control and different embeddings without it.

Step 2 must match the baseline, not merely be reasonable. Accumulating raw
intensity where the baseline accumulates its square root changes the intensity
scale as well as the mass axis, which reintroduces exactly the kind of two-factor
comparison the control exists to remove.

\hd{The conditioning-parity check}
Given the arms of the ablation, one fixed spectrum, and two settings $c_1, c_2$ of
the acquisition covariates:

\begin{verbatim}
  for each arm a:
      y1 = predict(a, spectrum, covariates = c1)
      y2 = predict(a, spectrum, covariates = c2)
      response[a] = max |y1 - y2|

  fail, naming every offender, if any response[a] <= tol
\end{verbatim}

Choose $c_1$ and $c_2$ to differ substantially --- a low against a high collision
energy, two adducts, two instruments --- since an arm may legitimately be nearly
invariant to a small change. An arm whose response is zero is not a weaker model
but a different experiment: every contrast measured against it also prices the
information it could not see. That is the failure reported in S1, and it cost half
of what a two-arm comparison here would have attributed to the spectral
representation.

One caveat makes the difference between a real check and a vacuous one. FiLM and
adaptive layer normalisation are commonly initialised so that the modulation is
the identity at step 0, which makes an untrained model return exactly zero for
every arm whether or not the conditioning is wired correctly. Perturb the
conditioning parameters off their initialisation before testing, or test a trained
checkpoint.
